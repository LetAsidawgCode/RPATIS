This research template is a comprehensive guide for University of Nueva Caceres students and professionals

research paper writing journey. This format also equips you with the skills and strategies to navigate the research

provides step-by-step instructions.  
To ensure that your thesis or dissertation meets rigorous academic standards, this guide outlines the 
recommended structure and formatting by incorporating key elements from the Publication Manual of the 
American Psychological Association (APA), 7th Edition. In addition to adhering to APA 7th Edition guidelines, this 
template integrates best practices and insights from various other reputable scholarly sources, providing a 
comprehensive framework to support your research's clear, consistent, and professional presentation. 
Laying The Foundation 
Before writing, decide how you will structure your ideas. Some use outlines, while others write first and organize 
later. Regardless of your method, scientific writing demands a well-defined structure. Consider the information 
each section of your paper will contain. If you do not typically use outlines, list each part's key points. For 
collaborative work, discuss and agree on the content of each section.  
 
Writing Your Research 
● Express your ideas in grammatically correct, complete sentences that are easy to understand.  
● Aim for English to avoid confusion.  
● Strive for clear and concise writing. Keep it brief and straight to the point.  
● Avoid unnecessary jargon or fluff. Focus on getting your findings and conclusions across efficiently.  
● Prioritize presenting your research clearly, not flowery language.  
● Scientific terms have specific meanings. Hence, choose your words carefully to ensure accuracy. 
● If you struggle to write clearly, it might indicate a gap in your understanding of the topic.  
 
Style Considerations 
● Write simply, say plenty. Focus on delivering your message concisely and directly. Avoid unnecessary 
words and ornamentation.  
● Use an active voice. This can significantly shorten sentences and improve readability. 
● Technical language allows you to express complex ideas efficiently. This applies to approved acronyms 
and abbreviations as well.

Some Things to Avoid 
● You do not have to try to impress people by using words most people have never heard of. 
● Do not use colloquial speech, slang, or “childish” words or phrases. 
● Do not use contractions: for example, “don’t” must be “do not,” and “isn’t” must be “is not,” etc.  
 
Abbreviations in Scientific Writing 
Do not use abbreviations in the text except for units of measure. Always abbreviate these when using them with 
data (2 mm; 10 min.). Except for temperature units (F, C, K), never abbreviate units of measure when using them 
in a non-data context (e.g., "we measured length in millimeters"; "time was recorded in minutes"; "temperature 
was measured in F (or C)"; "100 years have passed since Mendel did...").  
 
Use of Past Tense 
Research papers narrate the story of a completed investigation. Therefore, consistently use the past tense 
throughout your writing, including the introduction, when describing your research activities, the questions you 
posed initially (hypotheses), and the anticipated results (expectations). Similarly, use the past tense when 
mentioning the work of others you cite. 
 
First vs. Third Person 
Scientific writing typically uses a third-person point of view. This means that when you write your research paper, 
avoid using pronouns like “I,” “we,” “you,” and “us.” The third person promotes a more objective tone, distancing 
the writer's personal opinions and biases from the research findings. It shifts the focus to the research itself, the 
methods used, and the results obtained. Lastly, it maintains a consistent and clear voice throughout the paper.  
 
How to Write the Third Person 
● Pronouns: Avoid using "I," "we," "you," "me," "us," and "your." Instead, use pronouns like "the 
researcher," "the authors," "this study," or the name of a specific researcher. 
● Voice: Use the passive voice sparingly. The active voice can be more concise and easier to understand 
(e.g., "The experiment was conducted..." vs. "Researchers conducted the experiment..."). However, the 
passive voice can be appropriate in some situations, such as when emphasizing the action itself rather 
than the actor (e.g., "Data was collected from 100 participants"). 
● Focus: The writing should focus on the research itself, not the researcher(s). 
● Tips 
o As you write, read your sentences aloud to see if they sound natural in the third person. 
o Pay close attention to the verb tense (typically past tense for completed research).

2.1 Qualities of a Good Abstract 
● Accuracy. Double-check that your abstract reflects the paper's content and purpose. Do not add 
information not mentioned in the main text. If your work builds on or replicates prior research, cite it 
using the author-date format. 
● Neutrality. Report the facts; do not judge or comment on the findings in the paper. 
● Clarity. Use clear and direct language. Opt for verbs instead of nouns (e.g., "investigated" vs. "an 
investigation of") and active voice instead of passive voice (e.g., "we present results" vs. "results are 
presented"). Use the present tense for ongoing conclusions or results and the past tense for specific 
manipulations or measurements. Statistical or mathematical details should follow formatting guidelines as 
stated in this manual. 
● Conciseness. Keep it short and focus on the most critical information, especially in the first sentence. 
Prioritize key points and avoid repeating the title. Aim for just 4-5 crucial concepts, findings, or 
implications. Include the specific words your target audience might use when searching for research like 
yours.

Considerations for Crafting Research Questions 
In qualitative research, the focus is on research questions rather than objectives or hypotheses. Unlike objectives, 
which specify concrete goals, or hypotheses, which predict relationships or outcomes, qualitative research 
questions guide an exploratory inquiry into a central phenomenon or concept. They enable participants to 
understand complex, contextual, and varied perspectives. Below are some key considerations suggested by 
Creswell and Creswell (2018): 
● Avoid Objectives and Hypotheses: Qualitative studies should not use research objectives (specific goals) 
or hypotheses (predictions). Instead, focus on open-ended research questions that explore the 
phenomenon. 
● Question Starters: Begin questions with “What” or “How.” Avoid “Why” because it implies cause-effect 
explanations, which are less suitable for qualitative inquiry. 
● Single Phenomenon or Dimension: Focus each research question on a single phenomenon or a specific 
dimension/aspect of that phenomenon. This approach allows for in-depth exploration of each part while 
maintaining clarity and focus. 
● Exploratory Verbs: Use nondirectional, open-ended verbs such as explore, describe, discover, seek to 
understand, reflect, or report to frame your questions. 
● Avoid Quantitative Terms: Do not use terms like effect, influence, impact, determine, cause, or relate, 
which imply measurement or causality.

3.5  Conceptual Framework 
The conceptual framework is a researcher-developed blueprint that outlines the key concepts or variables in a 
study and shows their presumed relationships. It guides the research process by organizing ideas, clarifying 
assumptions, and focusing the investigation on specific factors relevant to the research problem (Ravitch & 
Riggan, 2016). 
Typically based on a review of existing literature, the conceptual framework is tailored to the study’s context and 
often presented visually or narratively. It helps define the scope of the research, guides methodology and data 
analysis, and strengthens the coherence and validity of the study by clearly mapping expected relationships 
among variables (Scribbr, 2023). 
3.5.1 Commonly Used Conceptual Frameworks 
The Input-Process-Output (IPO) Model. This framework emphasizes the flow of information, materials, or 
activities within a system. It is widely used in fields such as engineering, computer science, and project 
management. (Adobe Business, 2023; Management Consulted, 2024). 
Input. This represents the raw materials, data, or information that enter the system. It can be tangible 
(e.g., materials for production) or intangible (e.g., user input in a software program).  
Process. This describes the activities or steps that transform the input into an output. It involves the 
methods, tools, and procedures used to achieve the desired outcome.  
Output. This is the final result of the process acting on the inputs. It represents the end product or 
outcome of the system. Outputs can be data, results, products, behaviors, or any element produced by the 
process.

models, and methods to provide a structured approach to examining data, whether qualitative or quantitative

Analytical frameworks help researchers define clear indicators and variables, maintain consistency throughout 
data analysis, and adapt the framework as new insights emerge during the research process. This flexibility is 
essential for deepening understanding and ensuring that findings accurately reflect the data.

operational definition specifies the exact procedures used to measure or manipulate a variable, ensuring that the 
research can be replicated and empirically tested. Similarly, Creswell (2014) emphasizes that operational 
definitions provide clarity on how variables are measured or manipulated, forming the basis for reliable data 
collection and analysis.

4. The Review of Related Literature and Studies 
The backbone of your thesis is a well-written literature review. The literature review is a systematic search and 
analysis of existing documents related to your research topic. These documents can be articles, books, reports, 
and other scholarly sources.  
The literature review is essential in research because it helps you understand what research has already been 
done on your topic and prevents you from repeating someone else’s work. It determines what has already been 
done that relates to your topic and provides the rationale for your research hypothesis. It gives indications of 
what to do to help you justify the significance of your study. It enables you to refine your research questions and 
provides a way for novice researchers to convince the reviewers of the proposal that they are knowledgeable 
about the related research and the “intellectual traditions” that support the proposed study. It also lets you 
discover research strategies and specific data collection approaches that have or have not been productive in 
investigations of topics similar to yours. Finally, the literature review provides a framework for establishing the 
importance of the study as well as a benchmark for comparing the results with other findings.  
A literature review is not just summarizing past research. It is about demonstrating how your research fits into 
the bigger picture, why it is important, and how it builds upon what has already been done.

4.1  Purpose of the Literature Review 
● Informs the reader about research closely related to your own study. 
● Connect your study to the broader conversation happening within your field. This means identifying gaps

● Helps you establish the importance of your study and provides a benchmark for comparing your results to

4.2  How Literature Review Might be  Different in Approaches 
In Qualitative Research, the literature review is typically exploratory and contextual in nature. There may 
be an angle or aspect of the problem where not much might be written about, or a portion of the 
population is understudied. The researcher seeks to identify gaps and underexplored angles to guide 
participant interviews or observations.  
In Quantitative Research, the literature review is more substantial in grounding the research questions or 
hypotheses. The researcher uses the literature deductively as a framework for the research questions or 
hypotheses, as well as a reference to justify the methods to be applied. This approach ensures that the 
study is grounded in existing theories and research findings. 
In Mixed Methods Research, the research combines quantitative and qualitative evidence to address 
complex, multifaceted questions, offering a holistic understanding by integrating the strengths of both 
paradigms. It is ideal for studies requiring generalizability (via quantitative data) and contextual depth 
(via qualitative insights).  
 
4.3  General Tips

● Well-researched topics. Stick to your specific question. Focus only on studies directly related to your 
specific question. There will be plenty of relevant materials available, so you do not need to go off on 
tangents. Be guided by your research objectives or variables. 
● Build your Bibliography. Use a literature matrix to organize sources systematically. Include columns for

● New or under-researched topics. Cast a wider net and include studies that connect to your topic. You will 
need more background information to build a foundation for your research and develop a strong 
hypothesis.

5. The Research Methodology 
Research methodology is a systematic and structured approach to understanding and solving problems through 
scientific investigation. Its purpose is to provide a clear and organized framework that guides researchers in 
collecting, analyzing, and interpreting data. Here are some key purposes of research methodology: 
● Define the Problem: It helps clearly define the research problem or question, ensuring the study has a 
specific focus. 
● Plan the Study: It provides a blueprint for conducting the research, detailing the steps and procedures to 
be followed. 
● Data Collection: It outlines the methods and techniques for gathering data, ensuring that the information 
collected is relevant and reliable. 
● Data Analysis: It specifies the tools and techniques for analyzing the collected data, allowing researchers 
to draw meaningful conclusions. 
● Ensure Validity and Reliability: It ensures that the research is conducted in a rigorous and systematic 
manner, enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of the results. 
● Replication and Verification: It allows other researchers to replicate the study and verify the findings, 
contributing to the body of scientific knowledge. 
 
5.1  Research Design 
The following are the most commonly used designs in the different approaches: 
Quantitative Approach 
● Experimental Designs. These designs aim to establish cause-and-effect relationships. Researchers 
manipulate an independent variable and measure its impact on a dependent variable. They often involve 
control groups that do not receive the manipulation for comparison (e.g., testing a new fertilizer on one 
group of plants and comparing it to a control group).

○ Common experimental between-group designs include Posttest-Only Control Group Design, 
Pretest-Posttest Control Group Design, and Solomon Four-Group Design.  
○ Within-Subjects Designs include Crossover Designs and Latin Square Designs. 
● Quasi-Experimental Designs. Similar to experiments but lacking random assignment to groups, 
Researchers observe naturally occurring groups or preexisting conditions (e.g., comparing the academic 
performance of students who live in different neighborhoods).  
● Correlational Research Designs. These designs investigate relationships between variables without 
establishing cause and effect. They measure two or more variables and analyze the strength and 
direction of the correlation (e.g., studying the correlation between sleep duration and academic 
performance).  
● Survey and Epidemiological Research Designs. These are both tools for gathering information about 
populations, but they serve different purposes and have distinct characteristics. The survey research 
design is a systematic collection of data from a population or sample through questionnaires. They are 
primarily used for understanding attitudes, opinions, behaviors, and experiences. It is also used to gather 
descriptive information about a population. Types of surveys include cross-sectional surveys and 
longitudinal surveys. Epidemiology is the study of patterns, causes, and effects of health and diseases in 
populations. Epidemiological research designs use surveys extensively but go beyond them by focusing 
on identifying risk factors for disease and investigating outbreaks of disease.  
● Ex-Post Facto Designs. This is also referred to as “after-the-fact” designs, which involve investigating 
relationships between variables that already exist or have already occurred. These designs analyze 
existing data or groups to understand potential cause-and-effect relationships. In these designs, 
researchers do not manipulate variables. Instead, they identify groups with pre-existing characteristics or 
experiences (independent variable, e.g., being male and female) and compare them to an outcome 
variable (dependent variable).  
Qualitative Approach 
● Phenomenological Research Design. This explores the lived experiences and how individuals perceive a 
particular phenomenon. In this design, in-depth interviews are the primary method of data collection, 
allowing participants to describe their experiences in detail. Researchers analyze the interview data to 
identify common themes and patterns that capture the essence of the lived experience.  
● Ethnography. In this research design, the researcher immerses themself in a culture or community over 
an extended period to understand their values, beliefs, and practices. Researchers use a combination of 
methods, such as observations, interviews, document analysis, and participation in activities. They 
analyze the data collected to develop a detailed description of the culture or community, including its 
social dynamics, power structures, and unspoken rules.  
● Grounded Theory. This design aims to develop a theory based on data collected from the field rather than 
starting with a pre-existing hypothesis. The researcher often uses interviews and observations, but can 
also include document analysis and other methods. In analyzing the data gathered, the researcher 
follows an iterative process of collecting data, analyzing it for emerging themes, and refining their theory 
based on new insights.

● Case Studies. This explores a single case (individual, group, event, or community) in depth. The data are 
collected through a variety of methods such as interviews, observations, document analysis, and artifacts. 
In this design, the researcher analyzes the data to develop a rich understanding of the case and its 
context. Case studies can be used to illustrate broader themes or generate new research questions.  
Mixed-Methods Approach 
The mixed-methods approach combines the collection, analysis, and interpretation of qualitative and quantitative 
data. These are often used in studies that require an understanding of not only “what” is observed but also “why”

● Sequential Explanatory Strategy. The purpose of this design is to use qualitative results to assist in

collection and analysis of quantitative data, followed by the collection and analysis of qualitative data.

interpretation phase of the study. 
● Sequential Exploratory Strategy. The purpose of this design is to use quantitative data and results to

study variables are not known, refining and testing an emerging theory, developing new psychological 
tests or instruments based on the initial qualitative analysis, and generalizing qualitative findings to a 
specific population. The design is characterized by an initial phase of qualitative data collection and 
analysis, followed by a phase of quantitative data collection and analysis. The priority is given to the 
qualitative aspect of the study. The findings of these two phases are then integrated during the 
interpretation phase.

be equal between the two methods. Quantitative and qualitative data are collected and analyzed at the 
same time, and integration usually occurs at the data interpretation stage.

Measurement Scale 
In survey questionnaires, Likert-type scales are commonly used to measure observations and attitudes. 
Likert-type scales typically contain four, five, or seven response categories, each with its own advantages and 
considerations. The choice of scale depends on the research objectives and the nature of the data being 
collected. The following provides a comparison of these scales based on the literature review conducted by Rahi 
(2017): 
● 4-Point Scale: This scale forces respondents to make a clear choice by eliminating the neutral option. It is 
useful when the researcher wants to avoid indecision and obtain definitive responses. However, it may 
also lead to frustration if respondents feel forced into a particular stance. 
Pros: Encourages clear decisions and reduces ambivalence.

Cons: This may lead to respondent frustration and potential bias from forced choices. 
● 5-Point Scale: This scale is comprehensible to respondents and enables them to express their views 
effectively. It includes a neutral option, allowing for more balanced responses. 
Pros: Easy to understand, provides a neutral option for balanced responses. 
Cons: May not capture subtle differences in opinions. 
● 7-Point Scale: Reliability is often optimized with a 7-point Likert scale. It provides a greater range of 
options, which can capture more nuanced differences in attitudes and opinions. This scale is particularly 
suited to electronic surveys. 
Pros: Captures nuanced differences and better correlations with statistical tests. 
Cons: It may be more complex for respondents to navigate. 
General Considerations: 
The key point is that the number of scale points can impact the accuracy and reliability of the data. According to 
the literature, Likert scales with fewer than five points or more than seven points can lead to less accurate data. 
This does not mean that a 4-point scale is entirely inappropriate, but it does have its limitations. It’s important to 
consider the research objectives and the specific context of your study when selecting the scale. 
● A 4-point Likert scale is best used in specific situations where it is important to force a choice and avoid a 
neutral response. 
● For research focusing on individuals' behavior, a 5- to 7-point Likert scale is generally recommended as it 
strikes a balance between providing sufficient response options and maintaining accuracy. The 7-point 
scale has been shown to offer better correlations with statistical tests, while the 5-point scale is easier for 
respondents to understand and use.

5.3.1  Population and Sample 
Population refers to the entire set of individuals or items that one aims to study, sharing common characteristics 
defined by the researcher's criteria. Conversely, sampling is the process of selecting a representative subset from 
this population to analyze and draw conclusions. This method allows researchers to measure the characteristics, 
beliefs, and attitudes of people effectively (Rahi, 2017). By focusing on a sample, researchers can make informed 
inferences about the larger population, facilitating a more manageable and efficient investigation. 
The population is composed of two groups: 
● Target Population. The entire group of people or objects wherein the researcher wishes to generalize the 
study findings (e.g., all low birth weight infants; all pregnant teens). 
● Accessible Population: the portion of the population wherein the researcher has practical access to the 
population; this could be a subset of the target population (e.g., all low birth weight infants in Naga City; 
all pregnant teens in the province of Camarines Sur).  
The sample is representative of the entire population. The sample must be of sufficient size to warrant statistical 
analysis. A sample is usually gathered from where they are accessible from the target population.  
Sampling refers to the statistical procedure that is concerned with the selection of a subset of individuals; it helps 
us to make statistical inferences about the population.  
The sampling frame lists all the elements or units in the population from which the sample will be selected. 
 
5.3.2  Sampling Techniques

Probability Sampling: It is a sampling approach in which each unit has an equal probability of being selected. The

2. Compute the required sample size.

Selecting the Participants in Qualitative Research.  Random sampling, common in quantitative research, is less 
useful in qualitative research. This is because qualitative researchers often do not know exactly who or what they 
need to study at the outset (the "universe" is unknown).  Instead, researchers strategically choose participants 
who can offer valuable insights. Qualitative research is more about understanding a situation in depth. It needs to 
strategically choose participants who can provide rich insights, not necessarily a random selection.  
A review of the few qualitative research publications that treat sampling issues at greater length identifies five 
major types of nonprobability sampling techniques for qualitative research. 
● Convenience sampling is a technique that uses an open period of recruitment that continues until a set 
number of subjects, events, or institutions are enrolled. Here, selection is based on a first-come, 
first-served basis. This approach is used in studies drawing on predefined populations, such as 
participants in support groups or medical clinics. 
● Purposive sampling is a practice where subjects are intentionally selected to represent some explicit 
predefined traits or conditions. This is analogous to stratified samples in probability-based approaches. 
The goal here is to provide for relatively equal numbers of different elements or people to enable 
exploration and description of the conditions and meanings occurring within each of the study conditions. 
The objective, however, is not to determine prevalence, incidence, or causes. 
● Snowball sampling is a technique that makes use of participants as referral sources. Participants 
recommend others they know who may be eligible. 
● Quota sampling is a method for selecting a number of subjects to represent the conditions to be studied 
rather than to represent the proportion of people in the universe. The goal of quota sampling is to ensure 
the inclusion of people who may be underrepresented by convenience or purposeful sampling techniques. 
● In case study samples, select a single individual, institution, or event as the total universe. A variant is 
the key-informant approach (Spradley 1979), or intensity sampling (Patton 1990), where a subject who is 
an expert in the topic of study serves to provide expert information on the specialized topic.

5.4  Ethical Considerations 
In any research project, considering ethical issues beforehand is essential. This means thinking about potential 
problems that could arise and how you will handle them responsibly. Following ethical principles ensures that your 
work is conducted responsibly and respects the rights and well-being of participants. The following are the basic 
ethical principles: 
● Respect for Persons. This principle provides extra protections for vulnerable populations (those who are 
economically or educationally disadvantaged, prisoners, or pregnant women) and individuals with 
diminished capacity (children or cognitively impaired persons) while respecting their self-determination to 
the greatest extent possible. This further emphasizes the autonomy and well-being of research 
participants. It means obtaining informed consent, protecting privacy and confidentiality, and respecting 
the right to withdraw from the study. 
● Beneficence and Non-Maleficence. This refers to each researcher’s responsibility to estimate potential 
risks as truthfully and accurately as possible before proposing any research. This principle stresses 
maximizing potential benefits and minimizing potential harm to participants. Researchers should ensure 
that the potential benefits of the research outweigh the risks.  
● Justice. The principle of justice requires that researchers select their samples fairly to ensure that some 
people are not being manipulated. Justice means that research subjects should come from the groups 
most likely to reap the benefits. It ensures a fair selection of participants and avoids exploitation or 
discrimination. Researchers should strive for their research to benefit all of society, not just specific 
groups.  
These core principles translate into practical considerations for research, such as: 
● Obtaining Informed Consent. Participants must have a comprehensive understanding of the research 
purpose, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw before consenting to 
participate. Informed consent involves the participant agreeing to join the study after receiving clear and 
thorough information about its nature. This includes a detailed explanation of the procedures, answers to 
all questions, disclosure of potential risks and benefits, assurance that consent is given voluntarily without 
coercion or force, and the right to withdraw at any time. Participants must be guaranteed privacy and 
confidentiality, and researchers cannot waive their liability. The consent form should be written in clear, 
accessible language appropriate to the participants' reading level, signed by both the participant and the 
researcher, retained for documentation, and the information should be verbally reinforced to ensure 
understanding.  
● Obtaining Assent. Assent or agreement of minor children ages seven and above is usually a requirement 
for their participation. To the extent possible, assent is also obtained from cognitively impaired subjects, 
both adults (e.g., adults with Alzheimer’s) and children (e.g., children with autism or learning disabilities). 
● Protecting Confidentiality. Researchers must keep participant information confidential and anonymous 
unless explicit consent is given for disclosure. This means protecting the participants’ identities. This is 
done by collecting data anonymously, identifying data by code numbers, reporting average or group

6. Results and Discussion

6.1  The PAIL-R Method

results; it stands for Presentation, Analysis, Interpretation, and Recommendation, or Present, Analyze, Interpret,

through a systematic process of presenting and interpreting their findings. By following this method, researchers 
can ensure that their discussion is thorough, insightful, and aligned with the study's objectives, ultimately 
contributing to the clarity and impact of their research.

● Briefly summarize the key findings from your results.

● Do not just repeat everything that is presented on the table or figure, but highlight the main points that 
are crucial for the discussion. 
● Focus on the key results that are most relevant: highest, lowest, and overall. 
Analysis 
● Go beyond simply presenting the findings.  
● Analyze what they mean in relation to your research questions and hypotheses.  
● Draw logical conclusions explaining why something might be happening based on the results. 
● Explore possible explanations or reasons for the results. 
● Use tentative language: "may be," "possible that," or "could be because." 
Interpretation 
● This is where you delve deeper into your results 
● What are the broader implications of your findings?

6.2  Presenting Your Findings 
Present collected data and the results of all relevant analyses clearly and systematically to provide a solid

6.2.2 Graphical Presentation 
Graphical presentation involves using visual representations of data, such as charts, graphs, and plots, to 
communicate information effectively. Common types of graphical representations include bar charts, line graphs, 
pie charts, scatter plots, and histograms. Each of these visual tools is designed to highlight different aspects of 
the data, making it easier to understand and interpret complex information. 
Commonly used Graphs or Charts 
● Bar Chart: Used to compare different groups or categories. Bars represent values through their length, 
allowing straightforward comparison across categories. Ideal for categorical data comparison. 
● Line Graph: Ideal for illustrating trends and changes over time or continuous data. Data points are 
connected by lines to show progression or fluctuation. For example, tracking exam scores over several 
terms or monitoring COVID-19 case numbers over time. 
● Pie Chart: Circular charts are divided into slices, each representing the proportion of a category relative to 
the whole. Best used to show percentage or part-to-whole relations with a limited number of categories. 
● Scatter Plot: Displays relationships between two continuous variables by plotting points on Cartesian 
coordinates. Useful for detecting correlations or patterns between variables. 
● Histogram: Similar to bar charts, but used to show the distribution of numerical data by grouping values 
into intervals or bins. Helpful in visualizing the frequency distribution of continuous data. 
● Box Plot (Box-and-Whisker Plot): Summarizes key distribution features like median, quartiles, and 
outliers. Widely used for comparing data spread and identifying skewness. 
● Area Chart: Emphasizes total and sub-component changes over time by shading the area under the line 
graph. 
● Heatmap: Uses color intensity to represent values in a matrix, revealing patterns, trends, or hotspots. 
● Bubble Chart: Extends scatter plots by using bubble size to represent an additional variable, useful for 
multivariate data. 
● Treemap: Visualizes hierarchical data with nested rectangles showing size proportions, suitable for 
part-to-whole relationships in large data sets. 
● Radar Chart: Compares multiple variables across different categories on a circular grid, useful for 
multivariate analysis like performance metrics. 
● Funnel Chart: Illustrates progressive reduction or flow, commonly used in sales and marketing funnels to 
track conversion rates.

Post Hoc Tests in ANOVA 
Post hoc tests are conducted after an ANOVA if the ANOVA indicates a statistically significant difference among 
the group means. While ANOVA tells you that there is a significant difference among groups, it does not indicate 
which groups are different. Post hoc tests address this by making pairwise comparisons between group means. 
Common Post-Hoc Tests 
● Tukey's HSD (Honestly Significant Difference) is suitable for comparing all possible pairs of means 
while maintaining the overall Type I error rate. It is commonly used when sample sizes are equal.  
● Bonferroni Correction adjusts the significance level to account for the number of comparisons being 
made, offering a conservative approach that reduces the risk of Type I errors.  
● Scheffé's Test is flexible and can be used for unequal sample sizes, making it suitable for complex 
comparisons beyond just pairwise comparisons.  
● Dunnett's Test compares each group's mean to a control group's mean, making it useful when one 
group serves as a standard or control against which all other groups are compared.

● Present each research question individually, followed by a detailed presentation of its corresponding 
findings, conclusions, and recommendations. 
● Summarize the key results that directly address each research question. 
● Draw reasoned conclusions based on the analyzed findings. 
● Offer practical suggestions derived from the conclusions, acknowledge any study limitations, and highlight 
directions for future research. 
 
8.  Bibliography 
The bibliography section provides a comprehensive list of all the sources cited and consulted during the research 
process. It is essential to properly document these sources to give credit to the original authors, avoid plagiarism, 
and allow readers to locate the referenced materials for further study. 
8.1  Format 
● Start the reference list on a new page after the text and before the appendices. 
● Label the reference list “BIBLIOGRAPHY” capitalized, in bold, and centered.  
● Double-space all referred list entries. 
● Use a hanging indent for all references, meaning that the first line of each reference is flush left, and 
subsequent lines are indented by 0.5 in.  
● Use the paragraph-formatting function of your word-processing program to apply the hanging indent 
automatically.  
● Arrange references in alphabetical order by the last name of the first author. If there is no author, use the 
title of the work for alphabetization.

● Author Names: Use the last name followed by initials for the first and middle names. For multiple authors, 
use commas to separate names and an ampersand (&) before the last author's name. 
Example: Smith, J. A., & Brown, L. T.

9.  Appendices 
Authors sometimes have extra information that is valuable in the paper, but could disrupt the flow of their paper. 
This information can be placed in an appendix. Use an appendix only if the information helps the readers 
understand the research, evaluate its quality, or recreate the study itself. Remember, any appendix material needs 
to follow ethical rules, such as giving credit for borrowed content, showing data accurately, and protecting 
people's privacy (especially if it involves pictures or videos of them).   
9.1  Supplementary Materials and Documentation 
Material that should be attached to the appendix: 
● Certificate of Authenticity (see Appendix J) 
● Statistician Certification (see Appendix T) 
● Research Contribution Form (see Appendix L) 
● AI Declaration Statement (see Appendix K) 
● Application for Research Ethics Review (see Appendix H) 
● Informed Consent Form/Assent Form/Parental Permission for Child’s Participation in Research (see 
Appendix E-G) 
Other examples of material suitable for an appendix are :  
● a list of stimulus materials (e.g., those used in psycholinguistic research),  
● a detailed description of a complex piece of equipment and  
● a detailed demographic description of subpopulations in the study and other detailed and/or complex 
reporting items 
● communication letters used during the conduct of the study 
● Raw data sets or detailed statistical outputs that underpin the analyses presented 
● Questionnaires, surveys, or interview transcripts are used for data collection. 
● Supplementary tables, figures, maps, photographs, or diagrams that add context or clarity 
● Sample calculations or formulas illustrating analytical methods 
● Ethical approval documents, consent forms, or correspondence relevant to the study 
9.2  Format 
● Begin the appendix on a separate page after the bibliography. 
● Include a cover page titled “Appendices” (centered, bold, uppercase) before listing the individual 
appendices. This page serves as an introduction to the appendix section.

● Give each appendix a label and a title.  
● If a paper has one appendix, label it “Appendix”; if a paper has more than one appendix, label each 
appendix with a capital letter (e.g., “Appendix A,” “Appendix B”) in the order in which it is mentioned in

● Each appendix should be mentioned (called out) at least once in the text by its label (e.g., “see Appendix 
A”). 
● The appendix title should describe its contents. 
● Place the appendix label and title in bold and centered on separate lines at the top of the page on which 
the appendix begins. Use title case (see Section _____) for the appendix label and title. 
● An appendix can be all text, or it can mix text with tables, figures, or even both. If it's just text, you can 
include headings and special equations to organize the information.  
● If an appendix contains text, write the paragraphs as regular, indented paragraphs, the same as in the 
body of the paper. 
● If a text appendix contains tables, figures, footnotes, and/or displayed equations, give each one a 
number preceded by the letter of the appendix in which it appears (e.g., Table A1 is the first table within 
Appendix A or of a sole appendix that is not labeled with a letter; Equation B1 is the first equation within 
Appendix B; Figure C2 is the second figure of Appendix C). 
● In a sole text appendix, which is not labeled with a letter, precede all table, figure, footnote, and equation 
numbers with the letter “A” to distinguish them from those of the main text. 
● All tables and figures within a text appendix must be mentioned in the appendix and numbered in order 
of mention. The tables and figures within a text appendix should be embedded within the text. 
● If an appendix consists of a table only or a figure only, then the appendix label takes the place of the 
table or figure number, and the appendix title takes the place of the table or figure title. Thus, if Appendix 
B is a table-only appendix, the table is referred to as Appendix B rather than as Table B1. 
● If Appendix C is a figure-only appendix, the figure is referred to as Appendix C rather than Figure C1. 
● If multiple tables and/or figures (but no text) are combined into one appendix, label and title the 
appendix, and also number and title the tables and/or figures within the appendix (e.g., Tables D1 and D2 
are two tables in Appendix D).
